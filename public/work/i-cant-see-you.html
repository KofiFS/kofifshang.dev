I Can’t See You

A multiplayer shooter played in total darkness, where sound is the only thing that finds you.

01Overview

In the dark, every sound is a confession.

Every footstep, reload, shot and spoken word gives your position away.

Two to eight players hunt each other with almost no light. You carry a pistol with a torch, flashbangs, a tape recorder that replays sounds and voices from wherever you throw it, a walkie talkie that keeps transmitting where it lands, and a dummy that drops a ragdoll copy of you to draw fire. The lobby sets the match, down to how many of each item everyone carries.

Because sight is nearly useless, hearing is the game, and that put the hardest engineering in the networking layer rather than the renderer.

01

The server decides who hears what

Every footstep, shot, bang, impact and voice posts a noise event to a server-side noise bus. A component on each player’s controller drops anything outside their hearing radius, which scales with loudness, muffles sounds through walls, and sends that client only what it actually hears.

On screen, the edge of the display lights in the direction of the sound, with a small blip at the exact bearing. Your own noises are hidden; your own speech isn’t, so you know when you’re giving yourself away.

02

A tape recorder that records real voices

Throw a recorder and it keeps looping its tape from where it lands. The tape holds two things: every noise event within 22 m, replayed through the matching sounds, and the holder’s actual voice.

While a take rolls, the holder’s machine captures the chosen microphone, resamples it to the voice-chat rate and Opus-encodes it one 20 ms frame per packet, because the engine’s codec wrapper corrupts multi-frame packets that contain a silent frame. Packets stream to the server, which relays them to every client; playback decodes into a procedural sound, up to 60 seconds.

03

Speech is a noise too

Voice chat alone wasn’t enough: talking had to show up on enemies’ screens. The controller meters the chosen microphone itself and, when the level passes a threshold, posts a voice noise at the speaker’s body every 0.4 seconds.

It also reads the voice engine’s talker record, because the built-in local-talking check is broken for the first local player. Replayed voices from recorders and radios post noises the same way.

04

A project that rebuilds itself

The game’s input actions, character and item Blueprints, controllers, game modes and test map are generated by an idempotent Python script run headless through the editor. Re-running it after a pull restores anything missing.

Weapons from a newer engine version couldn’t open in 5.5, so they were exported as FBX and re-imported by the same script with generated materials and a muzzle socket.
